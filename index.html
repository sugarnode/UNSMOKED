01: The Business Model

How vaping products are designed to appeal to young people.

E-cigarettes were originally developed for adult smokers trying to quit. But the way they're designed, priced, and sold raises questions about who they're really made for.

01

Flavour engineering

Mango, bubblegum, strawberry. Sweet flavours reduce the harshness of nicotine and make first experimentation feel easier. Research shows these flavours are particularly appealing to adolescents (Pepper, Ribisl & Brewer, 2016).

02

Product design

Many vapes are compact, colourful, and look more like tech gadgets than nicotine products. The UK government has acknowledged that these design features may attract children and young people (DHSC, 2024).

03

Price point

Disposable vapes are deliberately cheap, often just a few pounds. Research confirms that low prices reduce one of the last barriers to trying (Wang et al., 2026).

04

The "safer" framing

E-cigarettes are less harmful than traditional cigarettes for adult smokers, but "less harmful" doesn't mean "safe," especially for someone who never smoked in the first place (ASH, 2025).

In April 2026, the UK passed the Tobacco and Vapes Act, giving the government powers to restrict flavours, require standardised packaging, and regulate how vapes are displayed in shops (Hogan Lovells, 2026). The full impact of these changes is still to be seen.

02: Your Feed

How vaping became normal online.

Social media is one of the main ways young people encounter vaping, but the content usually only shows part of the picture.

01

Entertainment over information

On TikTok and YouTube, vaping content focuses on tricks, flavours, and aesthetics. Research shows positive vaping content online vastly outweighs health information (Smith et al., 2023).

02

Influencer marketing

Some creators feature vapes in lifestyle posts without clear sponsorship disclosure. UK studies found this is one of the first ways many teenagers discover e-cigarettes (Smith & Hilton, 2023).

03

Peer norms and screen time

A UK cohort study found that daily social media exposure more than doubles the risk of youth vaping, because it constantly reinforces the idea that vaping is normal (Laverty et al., 2025).

Your body is still developing. Here's what the science shows so far.

This isn't about scare tactics. It's about what researchers have actually found, including what we still don't know.

  • Brain development. The prefrontal cortex, which is responsible for decisions, attention, and impulse control, continues developing until around age 25. Nicotine can interfere with how the brain forms connections during this period (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2016).
  • It's not water vapour. E-cigarette aerosol contains nicotine, flavouring chemicals, and other substances that can affect the lungs and brain (NASEM, 2018). The claim that it's "just water vapour" is inaccurate.
  • A possible gateway. Evidence suggests young vapers are more likely to start smoking traditional cigarettes. One study found that nearly half of adolescent e-cigarette users had started smoking within two years (Aleyan et al., 2018).
  • We don't have long-term data yet. E-cigarettes have been widely used for about 15 years. No long-term outcome data exists for people who started vaping as teenagers. That uncertainty itself matters (Royal College of Physicians, 2024).

07: Who Can You Trust?

Not everything online is trying to help you.

Anyone can post health claims. Here's how to check what's reliable.

?

Who made this?

Is it from a health organisation, a company selling vapes, or an anonymous account? The source changes everything.

!

Where's the evidence?

Reliable information links to research or explains where the data comes from. "Trust me" is not a source.

£

Is someone making money?

If a post promotes a product, even subtly, there's probably a commercial interest behind it.

Does it show both sides?
